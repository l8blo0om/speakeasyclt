Instructor Guide

8-Week Curriculum

6 session themes · Each one self-contained · All of them building · One idiom woven through every class night

Not Basic Practical Game-Based Repeating Cycle Idiom-Anchored
01
TO vs. FOR — What's the Difference?

"I went to the store FOR buy milk" — every level makes this mistake. One of the most common errors in American English and the fix is simple once you see the pattern.

Idiom of the Night: "Cut to the chase" — Skip the long setup. Get to the point.
Vocabulary Drop
purpose benefit destination exchange direction

🐠 Fishbowl Topic

"What did you come to America FOR? What do you go to work TO do?"

Forces natural use of both in the same conversation. Inner circle speaks, outer circle earns $50 each time they catch a correct usage.

🎭 Skit

The Restaurant Order — Customer is confusing TO and FOR in their order. Waiter keeps getting it wrong. Manager has to "cut to the chase" and fix the whole thing. 3 people, 2 minutes, the class catches every mistake.


🎲 Game — Word Chain Bounty

Each person uses TO or FOR in a sentence. Wrong one = pay $50. Catch someone else's mistake = collect $100. Whole room becomes the teacher.

💬 Idiom Callback: "TO learn English or FOR fun? Cut to the chase — it's both. And tonight you got it right."
02
Present Perfect vs. Simple Past

"I have went to the store yesterday" — kills Spanish, Portuguese, Korean, and Arabic speakers equally. The distinction between experience and completed action changes everything.

Idiom of the Night: "Been there, done that" — This idiom IS the grammar lesson. Meta and perfect.
Vocabulary Drop
experience recently already yet ago just

🐠 Fishbowl Topic

"Have you ever done something that surprised even you? Tell us what happened."

Inner circle uses present perfect to introduce ("I've done something crazy...") and simple past to tell the story ("Last year I went to..."). Outer circle earns for spotting the transition.

🎭 Skit

The Job Interview — Interviewer asks "Have you ever managed a team?" Candidate answers in perfect, then tells the story in past. Second character keeps mixing them up. Class catches every mistake for $50 each.


🎲 Game — Hot Seat Jail

One person in the center. Rapid-fire questions from the room — every answer must use present perfect. Wrong tense = pay $100. Survive 60 seconds clean = collect $300.

💬 Idiom Callback: "Been there. Done that. And now you know the difference between when you HAVE been somewhere and when you WENT."
03
Conditionals for Real Life

Americans use conditionals constantly — at work, making plans, being polite. Just two structures: one for things that might happen, one for things that probably won't. The tense shift is everything.

Idiom of the Night: "On the fence" — You haven't decided. You could go either way.
Two Structures Only

Real: If I have time, I will... (it might happen)

Hypothetical: If I had time, I would... (it probably won't)


possibility consequence hypothetical regret what-if

🐠 Fishbowl Topic

"If you could change one thing about your life in America, what would it be?" vs. "If you get a promotion this year, what will you do?"

Facilitator asks both. Group notices why the tense shifts and what it signals.

🎭 Skit

Apartment Hunting — One person is on the fence. Landlord uses real conditionals to sell ("If you sign today, I will lower the deposit"). Roommate uses hypotheticals to warn ("If I were you, I would check the neighbors"). Classic real-life negotiation.


🎨 Game — Pictionary Conditionals

Draw the conditional scenario. Team guesses the word AND must say the correct conditional sentence to win the pot. Wager your dollars before the draw.

💬 Idiom Callback: "Are you still on the fence about conditionals? If you practice tonight, you'll feel better by tomorrow. If you had practiced last year, you'd already have it."
04
Phrasal Verbs — The American Secret

Textbooks don't teach these. Americans use them constantly. You cannot avoid them at work, with neighbors, or watching TV. 7 phrasal verbs introduced and practiced all night.

Idiom of the Night: "Get the hang of it" — This IS a phrasal verb. Meta and perfect.
Tonight's 7
  • Call off — cancel
  • Put up with — tolerate
  • Run into — meet by accident
  • Figure out — understand or solve
  • Bring up — mention a topic
  • Get away with — do wrong without consequence
  • Look into — investigate

🐠 Fishbowl Topic

"Tell us about a time you had to put up with something — or someone."

Every person in the inner circle must use at least one of tonight's 7 naturally. Outer ring earns $50 per correct use spotted.

🎭 Skit

The Office Meeting — Someone brings up a problem. The boss tries to figure it out. One coworker keeps getting away with doing nothing. HR has to look into it. Every phrasal verb from tonight's list must appear at least once.


🎲 Game — Pronunciation Bounty

Any of tonight's 7 used WRONG — wrong word, wrong form, wrong context — the catcher collects $100 from the person who said it. Makes the whole room listen to each other all night.

💬 Idiom Callback: "You're getting the hang of it. Run into a phrasal verb this week — you'll figure it out now instead of putting up with the confusion."
05
Articles — A, An, The, and Nothing

Nearly invisible in spoken English but marks every non-native speaker immediately in writing. There are no perfect rules — you have to feel it. This session builds the feeling.

Idiom of the Night: "In the same boat" — We're facing the same problem. We're in this together. (Notice THE.)
The Four Cases

THE — specific. Both people know which one.

A / AN — first mention. One of many. Any member of a category.

Nothing — general statements. Uncountable nouns. Plurals used generally.

"Life is hard" — not "The life is hard."


🐠 Fishbowl Topic

"Tell us about a problem you've had — at work, at home, anywhere."

Facilitator writes their articles on the board in real time as they speak. Group notices patterns together. No one gets corrected — just observed.

🎭 Skit

The Mistake Mirror — Two people tell the exact same story. One uses articles correctly. One doesn't. The class spots every mistake in real time for $50 each. The second telling makes the correct version feel obvious.


🎲 Game — One Word Story

Build a story one word at a time around the circle. Every time someone must choose A, AN, or THE — they must say it clearly and the group votes. Wrong article = lose $50. Right = earn $50. The story gets absurd. That's the point.

💬 Idiom Callback: "We were all in THE same boat tonight — not A boat, not boats — THE same one. You'll feel the difference now."
06
How Americans Disagree Without Fighting

ESL learners either stay silent (too polite) or sound harsh (too direct). American professional and social disagreement runs on softening language that nobody teaches. Tonight we build the whole toolkit.

Idiom of the Night: "Agree to disagree" — We both have our view and that's okay. This idiom names the whole session.
Tonight's Toolkit
  • "I see what you mean, but..."
  • "That's a good point — I'm just not sure about..."
  • "I hear you, and at the same time..."
  • "Have you considered...?"
  • "I might be wrong, but..."
  • "With all due respect..." ← and when NOT to say it

🐠 Fishbowl Topic

Safe but real debates: "Is it better to rent or own?" "Should kids have phones?" "Working from home — good or bad?"

Inner circle must disagree with at least one person using the toolkit. Outer ring earns $50 every time they spot a toolkit phrase used correctly.

🎭 Skit — Two Ways

The Bad Idea at Work — One person has a bad idea. Two coworkers must disagree. First run: they do it badly — blunt, aggressive, awkward. Second run: they use the toolkit. Group votes on which approach would actually work in America. The contrast is everything.


🎤 Accent Imitation Round

Repeat the softening phrases with an exaggerated American accent. "I heeear you..." — gets silly. That's the point. The mouth is still doing the work. Earn $50 for the best performance as voted by the room.

💬 Idiom Callback: "Sometimes we agree to disagree. But now you know HOW to do it — in English, in America, without burning the bridge or swallowing your opinion."

🔄 Second Cycle Rule

When a session repeats in weeks 3–4, the idiom stays the same but skit scenarios get harder, fishbowl topics go deeper, and students who were there the first time become teachers for newcomers.

🎓 When One Teaches, Two Learn

In the second cycle, open the fishbowl by asking returning students to introduce the topic before you do. They'll surprise you — and themselves. That moment is the whole point.

💵 Economy Reminder

Every activity has Speak Easy Dollars attached. Don't let the economy go flat — call out earnings loudly, show the pot growing, remind people what Boardwalk Battle is playing for.
